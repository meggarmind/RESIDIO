# Settings holds only what an administrator sets, not what they watch

The Settings module is limited to Settings and Reference Data. Surfaces whose job is to show live or historical system state — audit logs, cron job status, the notification queue, sent-notification history, the ownership-history backfill tool, and account approval — move out of `/settings/*` to a System section of the main sidebar under `/system/*`, with permanent redirects from their old paths.

The reason is that no grouping rule could be stated while Settings did both jobs. Settings had grown to six groups and roughly thirty-four links with no rule at all, and the absence showed: the WhatsApp operational console sat under "General & Preferences", the Gmail import connection sat under "Billing & Finance", and a group named "System Health" held things you look at while every group beside it held things you change. Any rule phrased in terms of subject matter breaks the moment a group is defined by the fact that its pages are read-only, and any rule phrased in terms of what a page does stops matching the way administrators actually look for a setting. Splitting the two concerns is what makes "group by subject" expressible at all, and it is recorded here because the split is what a future reader will find surprising — not the grouping.

There is a deliberate exception inside the boundary. A manual trigger that runs the rule immediately above it stays with that rule: "Apply Late Fees Now" belongs beside the late-fee configuration, and "Prune Data" beside the retention policy. Tearing those apart would leave an administrator setting a policy in one section and executing it in another, which is worse than the impurity. The test is whether the button runs the rule on the same page; a tool that merely happens to live nearby, such as the ownership backfill, does not qualify and moves.

Integrations are exempt from this boundary entirely, for reasons recorded in ADR-0005.

The costs are real and worth stating. Roughly ten routes change, so every relocation needs a redirect, an entry in `ROUTE_PERMISSIONS` for the new path, and its old entry retained — the redirect stub runs after middleware, so deleting the old entry widens who reaches it. Hardcoded links across the dashboard, one link embedded in an outage-alert email, several wiki pages, and two end-to-end test regexes all point at the old locations and must move in step. Against that, the alternative was to keep a section that cannot explain what belongs in it, which is what produced the problem in the first place.

One hazard deserves emphasis because it is specific to this codebase. Middleware is the only authentication gate for the dashboard; neither the layout nor the shell guards anything. When no `ROUTE_PERMISSIONS` key matches a path, the entire authorization block is skipped rather than defaulting to deny, so a `/system/*` page shipped without its own entry would be fully public, not merely under-permissioned. A generic `/system` entry and a structural test covering the new tree are prerequisites of the first relocation, not follow-up work.

## Amendment (2026-10-20, #912)

Role definitions and role-assignment rules now live with account administration under System, on the single "Roles & Access" page at `/system/accounts` (tabs Roles and Who can hold each role, shown only with `system.manage_roles`). This is a named exception to "Settings holds only configuration". Defining a role and giving it to a person is one task, and splitting it across `/settings/roles` and `/system/accounts` left administrators looking in the wrong place. The reasoning is the same as the manual-trigger exception above: the two halves of one task stay together. `/settings/roles` and `/settings/user-roles` redirect to the new page, their `ROUTE_PERMISSIONS` entries are kept (the redirect runs after middleware), and Settings keeps a "Roles & Permissions" link to the Roles tab.

## Amendment (2026-10-21, #939)

Roles & Access is configuration, and it lives in Settings at `/settings/access`. This reverses the `/system/accounts` location chosen by #912, by owner decision on 2026-10-21 (#791): who holds which role is part of setting the estate up, not live system state. The page moves whole, including the Pending sign-ups and Unlinked logins tabs that this ADR first sent to System as account approval. The five tabs and every `?tab=` value stay, including the pre-#912 names. `/system/accounts`, `/settings/roles` and `/settings/user-roles` redirect to it, and all three keep their `ROUTE_PERMISSIONS` entries for the reason given above. The new route carries the `/system/accounts` guard unchanged (`system.assign_roles`), so nobody gains or loses access, and the known #912 gap stays as it was: an administrator holding `system.manage_roles` without `system.assign_roles` is bounced. The System sidebar no longer lists the page. Live-state pages (audit logs, scheduled jobs, the notification queue and history) stay under System, so the rest of this ADR stands.

## Amendment (2026-10-28, #1272)

The "System" section of the sidebar is gone. Live state and reports now live in one sidebar area named **Oversight** (Reports and Monitoring), which has its own left menu like Settings. Oversight and Settings sit together in the last sidebar section, "Admin". The pages keep their web addresses (`/system/*`, `/analytics`, `/reports/*`), their guards and their `ROUTE_PERMISSIONS` entries; only the menu, the titles and the layout change. The Monitoring link group in Settings is removed, because Oversight is now the one place to look. The WhatsApp monitoring windows also leave Settings for Oversight (slice 3 of #1270). Owner decision, 28 October 2026 (#1270). The rule of this ADR stands: Settings holds what you change, Oversight holds what you watch. Do not restore the old System section as a "regression fix".
